I thank both reviewers for their reports. Reviewer~1 identified three specific points. \section*{Reviewer 1}

I thank the reviewer for the careful second reading and for the positive overall assessment.

\subsection*{Comment 1: what triggers rejection or fallback}

\begin{reviewer}
Proposition 5 and Section 6.8 equate read attenuation with abstention, although deletion and anti-atom insertion produce identical argmax predictions. Please clarify what actually triggers rejection or fallback.
\end{reviewer}

\response The reviewer is right. The previous text said that the anti-atom ``abstains'' because its read is shrunk toward zero. Attenuation by a positive factor does not change the argmax, so a decision taken from the read vector alone cannot distinguish the anti-atom from deletion. Our own experiment showed this: the argmax coincided in all five cases.

What triggers fallback is a test on the \emph{mass} of the read. This is the same rule the null-atom router of Section~3.4 already applied ($A(x)\ge\tfrac12$), but the previous text did not connect it to the anti-atom. The revision makes the connection explicit in three places.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Section~3.4.} The text now states that the decision is taken on $A(x)$, not on the read vector, because $M_x$ rescales the read without changing its argmax. In a signed space, $M_x$ is replaced by the net signed mass $\mu_x^{\pm}(\Omega_s)\in[-1,1]$. This equals $M_x$ when the space has no anti-atoms.
\item \emph{Proposition~5.} The statement now also gives the net mass. It is $Z_o/(Z_o+2a)$ after an anti-atom and exactly $1$ after deletion, for every query. The proof is the same computation with $v$ replaced by $1$. This identifies the operational difference precisely: under deletion the read is still a probability measure on the remaining records, so a mass test always answers from memory, however diffuse the fall-through read is. Near the deleted key, the anti-atom drives the net mass to zero and the test falls back.
\item \emph{The paragraph after Proposition~5} adds three points.
  \begin{itemize}
  \item The attenuation also matters without an explicit test whenever the read is added to another representation rather than decoded on its own. In the GPT-2 injection $(1-\lambda)h+\lambda Wz$, an attenuated $z$ leaves the base model's prediction in place, whereas the deletion read steers toward another record's target.
  \item A gate that tests top-1 key similarity, as in Section~6.4, would still fire on the anti-atom pair, because its key is the record's own. It must be evaluated on the net mass.
  \item Deletion in a store that already contains a null atom also abstains, but only when no remaining key is close to the query. The anti-atom causes a fallback near the deleted key regardless of the rest of the store.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

The experiment of Section~6.8 now records the net mass and the outcome of the test (\nolinkurl{src/atom_space_unlearning_engine.py}). The rerun reproduces every previously reported number exactly. The net mass is 1.00 after deletion for all five sensitive queries, so the test answers from memory each time. After the anti-atom it is $6.1\times10^{-5}$ to $1.5\times10^{-4}$, so the test rejects all five. The text also notes that a test on the read norm would not separate the two arms in this store, because the diffuse deletion reads have small norms (0.18--0.27). This is why the mass, and not the norm, is the right statistic. The sentence ``the output is uniform'' is kept, but the manuscript now states that a uniform output is not itself an abstention. The Conclusion and the corresponding highlight have been reworded in the same way.

\changes Section~3.4, last paragraph; Proposition~5 statement and proof; paragraph after Proposition~5; ``Anti-atom versus deletion'' paragraph in Section~6.8; Conclusion; Highlights.

\subsection*{Comment 2: paraphrase score above activation}

\begin{reviewer}
The paraphrase score is described as ``capped by'' activation, yet Table 6 reports 62.6\% score versus 53.8\% activation. Please account for successful base-model predictions on inactive queries.
\end{reviewer}

\response The reviewer is correct, and the statement was wrong. The read leaves the forward pass unchanged when its gate does not fire, so on those queries it scores what the unedited model scores. The unedited model already prefers the new target on 28.1\% of paraphrases.

To account for this exactly rather than by approximation, I scored each of the 1,000 paraphrases separately (new script \nolinkurl{src/counterfact_score_decomposition.py}). It uses the scoring function, store, threshold, and injection weight of Table~6 unchanged, and it reproduces every Table~6 entry for the base and read columns.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
Paraphrases (1,000) & Share & Read score & Contribution \\
\midrule
Gate fires & 53.8\% & 92.4\% & 49.7 points \\
Gate does not fire & 46.2\% & 27.9\% & 12.9 points \\
\midrule
Total & & & 62.6\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
On every one of the 462 unfired paraphrases, the read's outcome is identical to the unedited model's (zero mismatches). The bound that does hold is the firing rate plus the unedited contribution on unfired queries, $53.8+12.9=66.7\%$. Paraphrase \emph{argmax} is bounded by the firing rate (44.2\% against 53.8\%), because the unedited model's paraphrase argmax rate is 0.0\%. The manuscript now states both. The same decomposition explains the neighborhood cost. On the 16.1\% of neighborhood prompts where the gate fires, the neighborhood score falls from 78.3\% (unedited) to 59.6\%. Elsewhere it is unchanged at 70.0\%, which gives the 3.0-point difference reported in Table~6.

The same incorrect phrase appeared in two further places, which are corrected. In the GPT-2 XL paragraph, the paraphrase score (57.1\%) also exceeds the firing rate; the bound is now stated for argmax (39.5\%). In the $N=50$ steering paragraph, the paraphrase score is 66\% against 58\% activation, while the argmax accuracy is 56\%.

\changes Paragraph after Table~6 (Section~6.6); ``Scale'' and ``CounterFact steering at $N=50$'' paragraphs of Section~6.9; new script; summary artifact \nolinkurl{results/counterfact_score_decomposition_gpt2.json}, with the per-query outcomes under \nolinkurl{results/details/counterfact_unified_outputs/}.

\section*{Reviewer 4}

I thank the reviewer for the report. I respond to each point. Where I disagree, I try to say exactly what the manuscript claims, so that the editor can judge the disagreement.

\subsection*{Comments 1 and 6: no new algorithm or system; a shared vocabulary rather than a better method}

\begin{reviewer}
Your work does not present a new algorithm or system. You explicitly call it ``a change of notation rather than a new algorithm,'' which weakens methodological novelty. [\dots] The paper offers a ``shared vocabulary,'' not a demonstrably better retrieval, routing, or editing method.
\end{reviewer}

\response The reviewer quotes the manuscript accurately, and I do not claim a new editing or retrieval method. The phrase ``a change of notation rather than a new algorithm'' refers to the read operator: under a counting measure it is exactly a softmax-weighted sum. The phrase does not describe the paper's contribution, which consists of results stated with that operator and measured on established data.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Null atoms} turn the activation threshold of gated retrieval into the zero-temperature limit of a smooth, calibratable abstention mechanism (Proposition~1). Applied to a trained tri-space router without retraining, it abstains on every far out-of-scope query and on 72.7\% of near misses, with every answered in-scope query correct (Table~8).
\item \emph{Anti-atoms} are shown to equal deletion plus a null mass (Proposition~5). This tells a practitioner exactly when retraction by an anti-atom differs from deletion and what a system must test to exploit the difference (Reviewer~1, comment~1).
\item \emph{A query-side certified radius} (Proposition~2) is computable at insertion time without a Lipschitz premise. It replaces a condition that, as a first-round reviewer showed, could never hold.
\item \emph{On CounterFact-500}, an outcome decomposition locates the entire gap between top-1 recall and activation in one bucket: correctly ranked paraphrases below the threshold. The decomposition also identifies the cause (distractor prefixes) and shows that a gate on the rank margin is better on both axes, at 70.8\% activation and 7.0\% false activation against 53.2\% and 16.1\% (held-out AUC 0.914 against 0.829).
\end{itemize}
The paper reports the rank-margin gate as a diagnostic, not as a method. It was evaluated on one store size, and its calibration depends on store density. I prefer to say this than to present it as a contribution the evidence does not yet support. The paper's claim is therefore analytical and empirical rather than algorithmic: it identifies what can be guaranteed about reads over external knowledge stores and measures how far those guarantees reach on standard data. I believe this is a legitimate contribution type. The Abstract, Contributions, and Limitations state it in those terms, so that no reader expects a new editor.

\changes None to the claims, which were already scoped in this way; see the response to comment~3 for the new Section~7.2.

\subsection*{Comment 2: integral and measure views of attention already exist}

\begin{reviewer}
The novelty of your work is insufficient. Integral/measure views of attention already exist and your paper mainly reformalizes known mechanisms.
\end{reviewer}

\response I agree that integral and measure views of attention exist, and the manuscript says so in the first sentence of the Contributions paragraph. It cites Martins et al.\ (attention over a general measure space, reducing to softmax under the counting measure), Tsai et al.\ and Katharopoulos et al.\ (kernel views), and Vuckovic et al.\ and Geshkovski et al.\ (attention as maps on probability measures). The claim made is narrower than a new view of attention. The claim is that three operations on the reference measure (separate normalization per space, signed atoms, and null atoms) let context attention, external retrieval, and expert routing be analysed together. Propositions~1, 2, and~5 follow from them, and none of these results appears in the cited works. The Limitations section states that the propositions are elementary and that their value lies in what they let one state and measure.

\changes None; the prior work and the scope of the claim were already stated in the Contributions paragraph.

\subsection*{Comment 3: no operational knowledge component}

\begin{reviewer}
Your manuscript does not have any operational knowledge component.
\end{reviewer}

\response I take the point that the knowledge-related content was spread across Sections~3--6 and was not presented as operations on a knowledge store. The substance is present. The corpus space is an external knowledge store, and in the CounterFact experiments it holds 500 subject--relation--object records. The paper gives, for operating it:
\begin{itemize}
\item a rule for inserting a record, with an insertion-time certificate;
\item a calibrated relevance decision;
\item an abstention and fallback rule;
\item two retraction operations and the exact relation between them;
\item a negative result for append-only updating;
\item an error-budgeted read of a large store.
\end{itemize}
The new Section~7.2 and Table~12 collect these operations in one place. Each row gives the operation on the measure, the result that governs it, and the evidence and measured failure mode in the paper. No new claims are introduced; the table summarizes results already reported. I hope it makes the operational content visible to a Knowledge-Based Systems reader.

\changes New Section~7.2 and Table~12; pointer at the end of the Introduction.

\subsection*{Comment 4: weak empirical gains; the gap to 91\% top-1 recall}

\begin{reviewer}
Your work has weak empirical gains. The gap to 91\% top-1 recall remains large.
\end{reviewer}

\response The 91\% figure is the read's exact top-1 \emph{retrieval} recall on paraphrases: the correct record is ranked first among 500 keys. The gap to 53.2\% activation is therefore a gap between ranking and the absolute threshold decision, not a retrieval failure. Section~6.4 measures it in three ways.
\begin{itemize}
\item The outcome decomposition (Table~5) shows that 37.8\% of paraphrases are ranked correctly but fall below the threshold. Only 8.4\% are genuine retrieval misses.
\item Re-scoring only the final sentence of each paraphrase, with keys and threshold unchanged, raises activation to 91.6\%. The distractor prefixes in CounterFact therefore lower absolute similarity much more than they disturb the ranking.
\item A gate on the rank margin, calibrated on the same held-out split, closes most of the gap. It reaches 70.8\% activation at 7.0\% false activation, against 53.2\% at 16.1\% for the absolute gate.
\end{itemize}
Under one scoring function on the same records, the gated read also has the highest paraphrase score of the four systems compared: 62.6\%, against 59.5\% for the rank-one parametric reference, 28.1\% for GRACE, and 28.1\% unedited. Its paraphrase argmax is 44.2\%, against 8.9\% for the rank-one reference. These are not small effects. I nevertheless do not present them as a state-of-the-art claim, because the comparison uses first-subtoken scoring and a GPT-2 scale that the Limitations section states. The purpose of the CounterFact study is to show where the gate fails and why, and to measure how much a better relevance statistic recovers.

\changes None; the analysis already appears in Section~6.4 and Table~6.

\subsection*{Comment 5: tri-space routing and steering are diagnostics}

\begin{reviewer}
Tri-space routing and steering are presented as diagnostics, not validated system contributions.
\end{reviewer}

\response This is correct and intentional. Both are labelled as controlled mechanism studies in the Abstract, the Introduction, and their own sections, and their limitations are stated where they appear and again in the Limitations section. They are not offered as validated systems, and the paper's conclusions do not rest on them. The routing study tests the null-atom abstention mechanism and the per-space normalization in a setting where routes are known. The steering study tests whether the relevance gate fires on the right prompts, and how large a hidden-state perturbation is needed. The substantive evidence is the CounterFact study and the unified comparison.

The reviewer may be interested to know that a separate study now in progress uses the null-atom gate of Section~3.4 over pretrained models as a working system (\url{https://github.com/mossaiby/RoHAS}). Its results are preliminary and are not part of this submission, and the routing experiments here remain diagnostics.

\changes None.
